Wi-Fi 2.4 GHz control: limitations
==================================

How it works: the PC sends controls over Wi-Fi to the drone's small ESP8285
chip, which passes them to the flight controller. That chip normally runs the
drone's ExpressLRS radio receiver; this project replaced its firmware.

1. The motors drown out the Wi-Fi (the main problem)
   - With the motors off, the link is strong and reliable.
   - Once the motors spin up, the link keeps dropping for a quarter to half a
     second at a time. It cuts out before the motors reach enough power to
     lift off, so the drone cannot actually fly this way.
   - The Wi-Fi chip and its tiny antenna sit right next to the motor
     controllers, which give off a lot of electrical noise.

2. Wi-Fi is the wrong kind of radio for this
   - Drone radios like ExpressLRS are built to push through that noise.
     Wi-Fi is not.
   - Slowing Wi-Fi down to make it more robust made things worse: the link
     failed almost immediately after arming.

3. The safety cut-off is strict
   - If no signal arrives for a quarter of a second, the drone stops and needs
     a restart. Making that window longer would hide the dropouts, but the
     drone would keep its last throttle for longer with no control.

4. Self-levelling was never on
   - A setting in the Wi-Fi firmware put the drone in a mode with no automatic
     levelling, so every Wi-Fi test flew without it.

5. The PC adds its own hiccups
   - Windows pauses Wi-Fi now and then to look for other networks or save
     power, and its timer makes the sends slightly uneven.
   - The laptop's built-in Wi-Fi sits next to the USB adapter and can
     interfere with it.

6. Updates are fragile
   - Firmware can be updated over Wi-Fi, but a bad update that stops the Wi-Fi
     from starting means falling back to a wired flash, which was unreliable on
     this board.
